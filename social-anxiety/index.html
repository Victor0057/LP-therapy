What is social anxiety?

Social anxiety is more than occasional shyness or feeling nervous before an important event. It can involve an ongoing fear of being judged, embarrassed, rejected, or closely watched in social situations. Someone experiencing social anxiety may worry for days before an event, feel intensely self-conscious during it, and replay the interaction afterward.

Social anxiety exists on a spectrum. Feeling nervous sometimes does not automatically mean you have social anxiety disorder. A mental health professional considers how persistent the fear is, which situations trigger it, whether you avoid those situations, and how much it affects everyday life.

Social anxiety is not a personal failure

People can want friendships, relationships, education, and career opportunities while still finding social situations difficult. Support is not about changing your personality or forcing you to become extroverted. It is about understanding the fear and making it easier to take part in the parts of life that matter to you.

Common signs and symptoms of social anxiety

Social anxiety can affect thoughts, emotions, physical sensations, and behavior. Experiences vary, but common patterns include:

  • Worrying about being embarrassed, criticized, rejected, or judged by other people.
  • Feeling very self-conscious when meeting new people or speaking in a group.
  • Avoiding phone calls, presentations, parties, meetings, dates, or unfamiliar social settings.
  • Rehearsing what to say before a conversation or struggling to speak spontaneously.
  • Reviewing conversations afterward and focusing on things you think you did wrong.
  • Blushing, sweating, trembling, a racing heart, nausea, or a shaky voice in feared situations.
  • Finding it difficult to make eye contact, eat in front of others, or do tasks while being observed.
  • Feeling relief after avoiding a situation, followed by frustration or concern about missing out.

These signs can also occur for other reasons and are not enough to diagnose a condition. If fear or avoidance is interfering with work, school, relationships, or daily activities, consider discussing it with a qualified health professional.

What can social anxiety look like in everyday life?

Conversations and meeting new people

You may worry about running out of things to say, sounding awkward, or being unable to respond quickly. Even a brief conversation can feel like a performance that has to go perfectly.

Work, school, and speaking in groups

Meetings, class discussions, interviews, presentations, or asking a question may bring intense anticipatory worry. You might know what you want to contribute but hold back because you fear making a mistake in front of others.

Dating, friendships, and social plans

You may want closer connections but worry about rejection, being misunderstood, or not knowing how to behave. Cancelling plans can reduce anxiety in the moment while making it harder to build the connections you value.

Being watched while doing everyday tasks

Eating, writing, using a phone, or completing a task while other people are nearby can feel uncomfortable when you expect others to notice or judge you.

Why can social anxiety become a cycle?

A common pattern involves anticipating a social situation, experiencing anxiety during it, and then reviewing the interaction afterward. Avoidance or safety behaviors may reduce distress temporarily, but they can also prevent you from discovering that a situation may be manageable without them.

  1. Before: you imagine what could go wrong and predict that other people will notice your anxiety.
  2. During: your attention turns inward toward your voice, appearance, body sensations, or every word you say.
  3. After: you replay the interaction, focus on perceived mistakes, and treat uncertain reactions as proof that you did badly.
  4. Next time: the memory can increase anticipatory worry and make avoidance feel safer.

This is one possible pattern, not a description of everyone. A therapist can help you identify what maintains your own difficulties and choose realistic ways to respond.

How is social anxiety treated?

Social anxiety is treatable, and support can be tailored to the individual. Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) specifically developed for social anxiety is a recommended psychological treatment for adults. CBT may help you identify unhelpful predictions, shift attention away from constant self-monitoring, and gradually test feared situations in a planned and supportive way.

Some CBT approaches include carefully planned behavioral experiments or exposure exercises. These are not about forcing someone into overwhelming situations. They are collaborative steps designed with a therapist, based on the person's goals and readiness. Other approaches, including acceptance and commitment therapy (ACT), may also be considered depending on the person's needs and preferences.

Treatment choices should be discussed with a qualified professional who can consider your symptoms, preferences, medical situation, and access to care. This page does not provide a diagnosis or a personal treatment recommendation.

Can online therapy help with social anxiety?

Online therapy can make it easier to begin a conversation with a therapist from a familiar environment. Depending on the provider and plan, care may include live video or voice sessions, secure messaging, structured exercises, or a combination of formats. Check the exact service details before enrolling because formats and availability vary.

Working remotely does not mean that therapy has to avoid the situations that feel difficult. A therapist may help you set goals related to conversations, work or school participation, relationships, or reducing avoidance. Any practice involving feared situations should be discussed and planned with the clinician rather than attempted as a sudden challenge.

What to discuss in your first session

  • Which social situations feel difficult and which feel more manageable.
  • What you fear might happen and what you do to cope in the moment.
  • Whether you avoid activities or experience worry before and after them.
  • How these patterns affect relationships, work, education, or daily routines.
  • What you would like to feel more able to do over time.

How to choose an online therapist for social anxiety

Before signing up, check the therapist's professional credentials and whether they are licensed to provide care where you live. Ask whether they have experience working with social anxiety and whether they use an approach such as social-anxiety-focused CBT when appropriate.

  • Credentials: confirm the therapist's license, qualifications, and jurisdiction.
  • Relevant experience: ask about experience with social anxiety, avoidance, fear of judgment, and related concerns.
  • Treatment approach: ask how goals are set, how progress is reviewed, and whether any between-session exercises are collaborative.
  • Communication: confirm whether the service offers video, voice, messaging, or other formats, and what each includes.
  • Cost and terms: review the full price, billing schedule, renewal terms, cancellation policy, and discount conditions.
  • Privacy and suitability: read the privacy policy and ask what support is available if online care is not the right level of care.

Frequently asked questions about social anxiety therapy

Can therapy help with social anxiety?

Therapy can help people understand social fears and practise new ways of responding. Social-anxiety-focused CBT is a recommended treatment for adults. The right plan and outcomes vary from person to person.

What type of therapy is commonly used for social anxiety?

Individual CBT specifically developed for social anxiety is a recommended psychological treatment for adults. A qualified clinician can explain whether it is appropriate and discuss other options based on your needs.

Is social anxiety the same as being shy?

No. Shyness is a personality tendency or feeling that may occur in some situations. Social anxiety involves fear and distress that can lead to avoidance or interfere with daily life. Only a qualified professional can assess whether symptoms meet criteria for a disorder.

Can I start online therapy if video calls make me anxious?

Some providers offer more than one communication format, but options differ by plan and location. Check directly with the provider to confirm whether messaging or voice options are available before choosing a service.

Does online therapy mean I will never have to face social situations?

No. Therapy may help you work toward situations that matter to you in manageable, planned steps. A therapist should collaborate with you rather than pressure you into situations you are not ready to approach.

How do I know if I should seek professional help?

Consider speaking with a professional if fear of judgment or social situations is persistent, distressing, or limiting your relationships, education, work, or everyday activities. You do not need to diagnose yourself before asking for help.
